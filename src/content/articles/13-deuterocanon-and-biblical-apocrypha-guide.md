## Historical Terminology: Protocanon, Deuterocanon, and Apocrypha

To navigate these texts with scholarly precision, one must understand the three primary terms used across church history:

### 1. Protocanonical Books (The "First Canon")
The term *Protocanonical* (from the Greek *protos*, meaning "first") refers to the **39 books of the Old Testament** whose canonicity was universally acknowledged by the Jewish community in Hebrew and accepted without dispute across all Christian traditions (Genesis through Malachi). These correspond exactly to the 24 books of the Hebrew Tanakh (which combined 1 & 2 Samuel, 1 & 2 Kings, 1 & 2 Chronicles, Ezra-Nehemiah, and the Twelve Minor Prophets into single scrolls).

### 2. Deuterocanonical Books (The "Second Canon")
Coined in 1566 by the Catholic theologian and Jewish convert Sixtus of Siena, the term *Deuterocanonical* (from Greek *deuteros*, meaning "second") does **not** signify an inferior or secondary degree of divine inspiration. Rather, it reflects **chronology of recognition**—books whose canonicity was accepted by the church at a later stage after initial discussion in the patristic era.

These seven books (plus additions to Esther and Daniel) are:
1. **Tobit**
2. **Judith**
3. **Wisdom of Solomon**
4. **Sirach (Ecclesiasticus / Wisdom of Jesus Son of Sirach)**
5. **Baruch** (including the Letter of Jeremiah)
6. **1 Maccabees**
7. **2 Maccabees**
8. **Additions to Daniel** (Prayer of Azariah, Song of the Three Young Men, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon)
9. **Additions to Esther** (Six Greek additions providing explicitly prayers and divine names)

### 3. The Apocrypha (The "Hidden" or "Ecclesiastical" Books)
Derived from the Greek word *apokryphos* (meaning "hidden" or "concealed"), the term was historically used by early church fathers like St. Jerome to designate books that were not found in the original Hebrew Bible (*Hebraica Veritas*). 

In Reformation theology, following the Thirty-Nine Articles of the Church of England (Article VI):
> *"And the other Books (as Hierome saith) the Church doth read for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine."*

Protestant scholars therefore refer to these works as the **Apocrypha**—honoring them as immensely valuable historical and ethical documents that provide the indispensable bridge between the Old and New Testaments.

## The Great Historical Debate: The Septuagint vs. the Hebrew Veritas

How did this difference in book counts arise in the first place? The root of the distinction traces back to two distinct manuscript streams in the ancient world:

### 1. The Greek Septuagint (LXX) Tradition in Alexandria
As examined in our [Digital Scripture Mission Guide](/articles/send-the-word-out-digital-scripture-mission/), during the third and second centuries BC, Jewish scholars in Alexandria, Egypt, translated the Hebrew scriptures into Greek for the vast diaspora of Greek-speaking Jews throughout the Hellenistic world. In doing so, they also translated and bound together contemporary Jewish historical and wisdom literature composed in Greek or Aramaic.

When the early Christian church spread across the Roman Empire, the Apostles and early church fathers—such as Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Tertullian—quoted overwhelmingly from the Greek Septuagint. Because the Septuagint circulated with these historical books bound alongside the prophets and the law, early Gentile Christians naturally received them as part of their sacred inheritance.

### 2. St. Jerome vs. St. Augustine (4th Century AD)
The first great scholarly debate regarding these books erupted in the late fourth century between the two greatest intellectual giants of the Western Church:
- **St. Jerome (c. 347–420):** While translating the Latin Vulgate in Bethlehem, Jerome consulted Jewish rabbis and discovered that these books did not exist in the standardized Hebrew canon. Jerome coined the term "Apocrypha" and argued that the Church should follow the *Hebraica Veritas* (Hebrew Truth)—holding the Hebrew 39 books as canonical for doctrine, while treating the extra Greek books as useful for edification but not authoritative for faith.
- **St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430):** Augustine argued passionately against Jerome. He maintained that the universal reception and liturgical use of the Septuagint by the apostolic church conferred divine authority upon the broader collection. Augustine successfully urged the North African Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) to list the 46-book Old Testament as canonical.

Jerome bowed to ecclesiastical consensus and translated the Deuterocanonical books into his Latin Vulgate, where they remained integrated for the next eleven hundred years throughout Western Christendom.

### 3. The Protestant Reformation and the Council of Trent
During the Protestant Reformation, Martin Luther, John Calvin, and the English Reformers returned to Jerome’s original distinction. Because certain Catholic doctrines (specifically prayers for the dead in 2 Maccabees 12:43–46 and salvation through almsgiving in Tobit 12:8–9) were supported primarily by texts outside the Hebrew canon, the Reformers insisted on *Sola Scriptura*—basing Christian dogma strictly upon the 39 Hebrew books recognized by Jesus and the Apostles.

In his 1534 German Bible, Martin Luther moved these books to a separate section placed between the Old and New Testaments, under the famous heading:
> ***Apocrypha:*** *These are books which are not held equal to the Sacred Scriptures, and yet are useful and good to read.*

The Council of Trent responded in 1546 during its Fourth Session by dogmatically defining the 46-book Old Testament as canonical scripture for the Catholic Church, pronouncing an anathema upon anyone who refused to receive them as sacred and canonical.

Meanwhile, the Authorized King James Bible of 1611, the Geneva Bible of 1560, and the Bishops' Bible of 1568 all continued to print the Apocrypha as an essential historical section between the Testaments. It was not until the early nineteenth century that British and American Bible societies began omitting the Apocrypha from popular print runs to reduce printing costs and shipping weights.

## Profile of the Major Deuterocanonical & Apocryphal Books

To read these texts with understanding, let us examine the themes and contents of the primary books available in AduBible’s dedicated sanctuary:

### 1. The Books of the Maccabees (1 & 2 Maccabees)
- **Historical Significance:** Essential historical chronicles recounting the heroic Jewish revolt led by Judas Maccabeus against the Syrian Greek tyrant Antiochus IV Epiphanes (c. 167–160 BC). Antiochus had desecrated the Jerusalem Temple by sacrificing a pig on the altar, erecting a statue of Zeus, and outlawing circumcision upon pain of death.
- **New Testament Connection:** The Maccabean purification and re-dedication of the Temple in 164 BC instituted the **Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah)**. In John 10:22–23, Jesus is found walking in Solomon's Porch at the Jerusalem Temple during this exact festival! Without 1 Maccabees, a New Testament reader cannot understand the background of John 10, the origin of the Sadducean and Pharisaic parties, or the Jewish expectation of a warrior-king Messiah.

### 2. The Wisdom of Solomon (Book of Wisdom)
- **Author & Theme:** Written in elevated Greek prose (traditionally attributed to King Solomon, composed in Alexandria c. 50 BC), this philosophical and theological masterpiece contrasts godly wisdom (*Sophia*) with the folly of idolatry.
- **Theological Depth:** Contains some of the most profound pre-Christian descriptions of the immortality of the soul and the righteous suffering servant. Wisdom 2:12–20 contains an astonishing prophetic portrait of an innocent righteous man condemned to a shameful death by the wicked because he calls God his Father—a passage quoted and alluded to by Matthew in his crucifixion narrative (Matthew 27:43).

### 3. Sirach (Ecclesiasticus / The Wisdom of Jesus Ben Sira)
- **Author & Theme:** Composed in Hebrew in Jerusalem around 180 BC by Jesus the son of Sirach, and translated into Greek by his grandson in Egypt. Sirach is an exhaustive manual of practical, ethical, and spiritual wisdom for daily living, family relationships, friendship, speech, and the fear of the Lord.
- **Theological Impact:** Sirach was the most widely read ethical textbook in the early Church (hence its Latin name *Ecclesiasticus*, "The Church Book"). The Epistle of James contains dozens of direct literary echoes and parallels to Ben Sira’s teachings on the control of the tongue, humility, and the vanity of wealth.

### 4. Tobit and Judith
- **Tobit:** A charming and pious religious narrative set in Nineveh during the Assyrian captivity, emphasizing steadfast faith during exile, charity to the poor, honorable burial of the dead, marital purity, and the ministry of the holy archangel Raphael.
- **Judith:** A dramatic historical narrative recounting how a devout Jewish widow named Judith delivers her besieged city of Bethulia from the invading Assyrian general Holofernes, illustrating that God delivers His covenant people not through military might, but through prayer, fasting, and righteous courage.

### 5. The Additions to Daniel and Esther
- **Additions to Daniel:** Three remarkable texts:
  - *The Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Young Men:* An inspiring liturgical prayer sung by Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego inside the Babylonian fiery furnace.
  - *Susanna:* A timeless courtroom drama of an innocent righteous woman vindicated by the young prophet Daniel against corrupt religious elders.
  - *Bel and the Dragon:* Two satirical apologetic tales demonstrating the folly of Babylonian idol worship.
- **Additions to Esther:** Six extensive sections that insert explicit prayers, tears, and the holy name of God into the Book of Esther, addressing the famous curiosity that the canonical Hebrew text never explicitly mentions God's name.

### The Theological Evolution of Resurrection: 2 Maccabees Chapter 7

One of the most profound theological contributions of the Deuterocanonical literature is its vivid witness to the development of the **doctrine of physical, bodily resurrection**.

In the earliest books of the Hebrew Old Testament, life after death is often portrayed in shadowy, restrained terms as *Sheol*—the place of the dead where active praise of God ceases (e.g., Psalm 6:5; 88:10–12). While explicit prophecies of resurrection break through in Isaiah 26:19 and Daniel 12:2, it is within the Second Temple period that the hope of bodily resurrection becomes the blazing centerpiece of Jewish martyrdom theology.

In **2 Maccabees chapter 7**, we read the harrowing historical account of seven Jewish brothers and their heroic mother who were arrested and tortured to death by Antiochus IV Epiphanes for refusing to violate the Mosaic law by eating swine's flesh. 

As the second brother is being flayed alive, he cries out to the tyrant with his dying breath:
> *"You accursed wretch! You dismiss us from this present life, but the King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life, because we have died for His laws!"* (2 Maccabees 7:9)

The third brother puts out his tongue and stretches out his hands to be amputated, declaring:
> *"I got these from Heaven, and because of His laws I disdain them, and from Him I hope to get them back again!"* (2 Maccabees 7:11)

This magnificent chapter is the direct historical background behind Hebrews 11:35: *"Others were tortured, not accepting deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection."* 

By reading 2 Maccabees in parallel with Hebrews 11 on AduBible, the Christian student understands that the early Church did not invent the doctrine of resurrection in a vacuum; they recognized its ultimate, definitive vindication in the bodily resurrection of the crucified Lord Jesus Christ from the tomb!

### Daniel’s "Abomination of Desolation" and 1 Maccabees

Another indispensable cross-testament linkage is the phrase **"the abomination of desolation"** (*to bdelygma tēs erēmōseōs*), spoken of by Daniel the prophet and referenced directly by the Lord Jesus Christ in His Olivet Discourse:
> *"Therefore when you see the 'abomination of desolation,' spoken of by Daniel the prophet, standing in the holy place (whoever reads, let him understand)..."* (Matthew 24:15)

What was this abomination? In 1 Maccabees 1:54, the historical fulfillment of Daniel’s prophecy under Antiochus IV Epiphanes is explicitly recorded:
> *"Now on the fifteenth day of the month Chislev, in the one hundred and forty-fifth year, they erected a desolating sacrilege upon the altar of burnt offering. They also built altars in the surrounding cities of Judah..."*

Antiochus erected an altar to the pagan god Zeus Olympios directly on top of the sacred brazen altar of the Lord in Jerusalem and sacrificed an unclean swine upon it. By reading 1 Maccabees on AduBible, the student grasps the visceral horror of this event, allowing them to comprehend why Jesus used this exact terminology to prophesy the future destruction of the Second Temple by Roman legions in AD 70.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did Jesus or the New Testament Apostles quote the Apocrypha?
While Jesus and the Apostles never introduce an Apocryphal quote with the authoritative formula *"It is written"* (*gegraptai*), the New Testament contains dozens of unmistakable allusions, conceptual parallels, and shared vocabulary with the Septuagint Deuterocanon (e.g., Hebrews 11:35 alluding to the martyrs in 2 Maccabees 7; Romans 1:18–32 drawing upon Wisdom 13–14; and James echoing Sirach throughout).

### Why did the 1611 King James Bible include the Apocrypha?
The 1611 King James translators were following the standard practice of the Protestant Reformation. All major English Bibles of that era (including the 1535 Coverdale Bible, the 1537 Matthew Bible, the 1539 Great Bible, and the 1560 Geneva Bible) included the Apocrypha as an essential historical appendix between the Old and New Testaments for historical and ethical instruction.

### What is the difference between the Deuterocanon and the Pseudepigrapha?
The **Deuterocanon** refers to the specific books included in the Greek Septuagint and Latin Vulgate canons (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Maccabees, etc.). The **Pseudepigrapha** refers to a broader collection of ancient Jewish religious writings from the same era that were attributed pseudonymously to ancient biblical patriarchs (such as *1 Enoch*, *Book of Jasher*, *Book of Jubilees*, and *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*), which were never accepted into the standard Western or Eastern biblical canons (though 1 Enoch is canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church).
