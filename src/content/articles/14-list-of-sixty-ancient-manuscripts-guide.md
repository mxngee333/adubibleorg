## The Discovery and Meaning of the "List of Sixty"

The document known to church historians as the **List of Sixty** was first published in modern scholarly circles by the German philologist J. A. Fabricius in his *Bibliotheca Graeca* (1712), and later critically analyzed by Theodor Zahn and B. F. Westcott in their monumental studies on the history of the New Testament canon.

### The Structure of the Ancient Greek Catalog:
The ancient Greek manuscript text is arranged in three distinct sequential tiers:

#### Tier 1: The Sixty Canonical Books (*Ta Hexēkonta Biblia*)
The catalog begins by listing the undisputed books of the Old and New Testaments. In ancient scribal enumeration, books like 1 and 2 Samuel were counted as one book, 1 and 2 Kings as one book, and the Twelve Minor Prophets as a single volume ("The Twelve"). 

The list of sixty concludes with the New Testament:
- The Four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John)
- The Acts of the Apostles
- The Fourteen Epistles of Paul (including Hebrews)
- The Seven General Epistles (James, 1 & 2 Peter, 1, 2, & 3 John, Jude)
*Note:* In this specific Byzantine list, the Book of Revelation was temporarily omitted from the primary sixty—reflecting the historical Eastern hesitation toward the Apocalypse during the fourth through seventh centuries before its universal canonical reception.

#### Tier 2: The Books Outside the Sixty (*Exō tōn Hexēkonta*)
Immediately following the canonical enumeration, the ancient scribe writes:
> ***"And the following books are outside the sixty:"***

This tier lists the revered **Deuterocanonical books** of the Septuagint:
1. The Wisdom of Solomon
2. The Wisdom of Jesus son of Sirach
3. 1, 2, 3, and 4 Maccabees
4. Judith
5. Tobit

#### Tier 3: The Ancient Apocrypha (*Kai hosa apokrypha*)
Finally, the document records the ancient Jewish and early Christian writings that circulated widely among the churches:
1. **The Book of Adam**
2. **The Book of Enoch (1 Enoch)**
3. **The Book of Lamech**
4. **The Patriarchs (Testament of the Twelve Patriarchs)**
5. **The Prayer of Joseph**
6. **The Eldad and Modad**
7. **The Testament of Moses (Assumption of Moses)**
8. **The Apocalypse of Elijah**
9. **The Apocalypse of Zephaniah**
10. **The Didache (Teaching of the Apostles)**
11. **The Shepherd of Hermas**
12. **The Epistle of Barnabas**
13. **The Epistles of Clement**

This ancient tripartite classification demonstrates that the early Church possessed a sophisticated, discerning taxonomy. They did not lump all non-canonical writings into a single trash bin of heresy; rather, they clearly distinguished between:
- Infallible, divine canonical scripture (Tier 1)
- Edifying ecclesiastical and historical deuterocanon (Tier 2)
- Ancient apostolic and Jewish historical writings useful for background, history, and moral instruction (Tier 3)
- Gnostic and heretical forgeries, which were completely banned and discarded.

## The Great Uncial Bibles: What Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus Reveal

The physical evidence preserved in our oldest surviving Greek Bible codices provides overwhelming archaeological proof of how the early Church read these books.

### 1. Codex Sinaiticus (א / 01, c. AD 330–360)
Discovered by Constantin von Tischendorf at Saint Catherine’s Monastery at the foot of Mount Sinai in the nineteenth century and now preserved in the British Library, **Codex Sinaiticus** is the oldest complete manuscript of the New Testament in existence. 

When you examine the physical parchment of Codex Sinaiticus:
- Following the Book of Revelation, the codex does not end.
- Copied by the same scribes, on the same vellum, with the same elegant four-column Greek uncial script, the codex includes the complete text of the **Epistle of Barnabas** and the **Shepherd of Hermas**!
- This physical reality proves that in the fourth century AD, the Christian community in the East held Barnabas and Hermas in such immense reverence that they bound them directly into their Great Pulpit Bible alongside the Apostles.

### 2. Codex Alexandrinus (A / 02, c. AD 400–440)
Presented by Cyril Lucaris, Patriarch of Constantinople, to King Charles I of England in 1627 and now preserved in the British Library alongside Sinaiticus, **Codex Alexandrinus** is the premier manuscript witness to the Byzantine and Alexandrian text traditions.

At the conclusion of the New Testament in Codex Alexandrinus, the manuscript contains:
- The complete Greek text of **1 Clement** (the Epistle of Clement of Rome to the Corinthians, written c. AD 96).
- The text of **2 Clement** (an ancient early Christian sermon).
- The table of contents at the beginning of the manuscript explicitly lists 1 and 2 Clement under the New Testament heading!

### The Four Golden Criteria of Apostolic Canonicity

How did the ancient bishops and synods determine which books belonged to the sacred canon of scripture, which belonged to the ecclesiastical "outside" books, and which were heretical forgeries?

Contrary to sensationalist popular myths (such as those popularized by Dan Brown’s *The Da Vinci Code*), the New Testament canon was not arbitrarily decided by Emperor Constantine at the Council of Nicaea in AD 325. Rather, the canon was the result of a long, organic, Spirit-guided recognition process across three centuries, governed by **four rigorous objective criteria**:

#### 1. Apostolicity (The Apostolic Origin Rule)
A book had to be written directly by an inspired Apostle (such as Matthew, John, Peter, or Paul) or by an immediate companion who operated under direct apostolic oversight (such as Mark writing the memoirs of Peter, or Luke writing under the guidance of Paul). Books composed in the late second or third century—such as the so-called *Gospel of Thomas* or *Gospel of Judas*—were rejected automatically because they lacked genuine apostolic pedigree.

#### 2. Orthodoxy (The Rule of Faith / *Regula Fidei*)
The teachings of the book had to cohere completely with the apostolic "Rule of Faith" preserved across the churches—affirming the true humanity and true divinity of Jesus Christ, His bodily resurrection, the goodness of physical creation, the triune nature of God, and salvation by grace through faith. Gnostic writings that claimed physical matter was evil or that Christ did not possess a real physical body were immediately exposed as heretical.

#### 3. Catholicity (Universal Ecclesiastical Reception)
The book could not be an isolated document known only to a single local sect; it had to be recognized, read aloud in corporate worship, and cherished by the universal (*katholikos*) body of Christ across Asia Minor, Greece, Rome, North Africa, Egypt, and Syria.

#### 4. Liturgical Traditional Usage
How had the churches actually worshiped for generations? The books that sustained the faith of martyrs in Roman amphitheatres, that were read aloud alongside the Old Testament prophets in Sunday assemblies, were recognized as possessing the divine breath of the Holy Spirit.

### The Muratorian Fragment: The 2nd-Century Roman Canon Witness

The earliest surviving historical catalog of New Testament books in the Western Church is the **Muratorian Fragment**, a damaged seventh-century Latin manuscript discovered by the Italian historian Ludovico Antonio Muratori in the Ambrosian Library in Milan in 1740, representing an original Greek text composed in Rome around **AD 170**.

The Muratorian Fragment provides extraordinary confirmation of New Testament canonicity:
- It lists the Four Gospels (Luke is explicitly identified as the third, and John as the fourth; the opening lines concerning Matthew and Mark are lost).
- It affirms the Acts of the Apostles, thirteen epistles of Paul, Jude, two epistles of John, and the Book of Revelation.
- Crucially, regarding the *Shepherd of Hermas*, the Muratorian author writes:
  > *"Hermas composed the Shepherd very recently, in our times, in the city of Rome, while his brother Pius was seated as bishop in the church of Rome. And therefore it ought indeed to be read; but it cannot be read publicly to the people in church either among the Prophets, whose number is complete, or among the Apostles, for it is after their time."*

Notice the crystalline clarity: the early Roman church deeply respected Hermas for private reading and spiritual encouragement, but drew an absolute line of demarcation between the inspired apostolic canon and post-apostolic writings.

## Deep Dive: The Apostolic Fathers and Ancient Pseudepigrapha

Let us examine the primary historical works available for study in AduBible’s dedicated **List of Sixty Sanctuary** (/list-of-sixty/):

### 1. The Didache: The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles (c. AD 70–100)
- **Historical Significance:** Lost for centuries and rediscovered in a monastery in Constantinople in 1873 by Philotheos Bryennios, the *Didache* is the oldest surviving Christian church manual outside the New Testament, composed while several of the original Apostles were still alive.
- **Content:** Divided into two distinct parts:
  - *The Two Ways:* A profound moral treatise contrasting the "Way of Life" (rooted in the Sermon on the Mount, sacrificial love, sexual purity, and the defense of the unborn against abortion and infanticide) with the "Way of Death" (marked by hypocrisy, idolatry, murder, and greed).
  - *Church Order:* The earliest detailed instructions on Christian baptism (by immersion in running water, or pouring water three times over the head in the Name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit), fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays, praying the Lord's Prayer three times daily, celebrating the Holy Eucharist, and testing traveling prophets.

### 2. 1 Clement: Epistle to the Corinthians (c. AD 96)
- **Author & Theme:** Written by Clement of Rome—traditionally identified as the companion mentioned by Paul in Philippians 4:3—from the church in Rome to the church in Corinth.
- **Theological Depth:** Addressed to a severe crisis in Corinth where young rebels had unjustly deposed faithful, godly presbyters. Clement appeals to humility, order, and love, providing one of the earliest extra-biblical affirmations of justification by faith alone:
  > *"And we, too, being called by His will in Christ Jesus, are not justified by ourselves, nor by our own wisdom, or understanding, or godliness, or works which we have done in holiness of heart; but by that faith through which, from the beginning, Almighty God has justified all men; to whom be glory for ever and ever. Amen."* (1 Clement 32:4)

### 3. The Shepherd of Hermas (c. AD 100–140)
- **Nature of the Work:** An allegorical, apocalyptic vision composed in Rome by a former slave named Hermas, consisting of five Visions, twelve Mandates (moral commandments), and ten Similitudes (parables).
- **Theological Impact:** Deeply revered across the Mediterranean (often quoted by Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen as scriptural). It deals extensively with repentance after baptism, spiritual warfare against demonic discouragement, and the building of the Church under the metaphor of a great spiritual tower constructed from living stones.

### 4. The Epistle of Barnabas (c. AD 100–130)
- **Thematic Focus:** A theological treatise written to Hellenistic Jewish Christians, warning them not to return to literal Mosaic ritualism. Barnabas utilizes extensive allegorical typology to demonstrate that the Old Testament sacrificial system, dietary laws, and Sabbath were divine types pointing forward to their definitive spiritual fulfillment in the crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus Christ.

### 5. 1 Enoch (The Ethiopic Book of Enoch, c. 300–100 BC)
- **The Jude Connection:** The *Book of Enoch* is perhaps the most famous ancient pseudepigraphon in Christian history. In the New Testament Epistle of Jude, verses 14–15, Jude explicitly quotes from 1 Enoch 1:9:
  > *"Now Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied about these men also, saying, 'Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of His saints, to execute judgment on all, to convict all who are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds...'"*
- **Canonical Status:** While Western and Eastern Orthodox churches excluded 1 Enoch from their canonical Old Testament, the **Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church** has preserved 1 Enoch as canonical scripture for over fifteen hundred years. 
- **Theological Value:** 1 Enoch provides the indispensable historical and apocalyptic context for understanding Second Temple Jewish angelology, demonology (the origin of demons as the disembodied spirits of the Nephilim giants from Genesis 6), and the heavenly title *"Son of Man"* utilized so frequently by Jesus in the Gospels.

No. The historic Church clearly distinguished between the 66 canonical scriptures (which are the unique, infallible, breathed-out Word of God) and ecclesiastical writings like the Didache or 1 Clement. ### Why does Jude quote 1 Enoch in the New Testament?
In Jude 14–15, the Apostle Jude quotes a true historical prophecy uttered by Enoch that was preserved in ancient oral tradition and recorded in the written Book of Enoch. Just as the Apostle Paul quoted pagan Greek poets in Acts 17:28 and Titus 1:12 to illustrate divine truth without endorsing the entire pagan book as scripture, Jude quoted a true prophetic statement from 1 Enoch under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit.

Because Ethiopia preserved the ancient Ge'ez translation of 1 Enoch while Greek and Latin manuscripts in the Roman Empire were lost or suppressed during barbarian invasions, 1 Enoch remained an integral part of their broader biblical canon.
