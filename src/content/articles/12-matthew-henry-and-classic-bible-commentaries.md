## The Life and Spiritual Genius of Matthew Henry (1662–1714)

To read Matthew Henry fruitfully, one must understand the man behind the pen. Matthew Henry was not a detached academic theorist sitting in a university ivory tower; he was a deeply pastoral, warm-hearted Nonconformist Puritan minister whose scholarship was forged in the furnace of pastoral prayer and suffering.

### 1. The Heritage of the Great Ejection
Matthew Henry was born in Flintshire, Wales, in 1662—the very year of the **Great Ejection**, in which more than two thousand faithful Puritan ministers were forcibly cast out of their pulpits in the Church of England for refusing to conform to the unbiblical liturgical impositions of the Act of Uniformity. His father, Philip Henry, was one of those ejected ministers—a man of profound learning and saintly godliness who converted his home into a private academy of prayer and biblical study. Growing up under his father’s loving tutelage, young Matthew learned to read Hebrew and Greek before he was ten years old.

### 2. Pastoral Ministry in Chester
In 1687, Henry was ordained as a Nonconformist Presbyterian minister and called to the church in Chester, England, where he served with unflagging devotion for twenty-five years. He was an indefatigable pastor: he preached multiple times every Sunday, led mid-week expository lectures, visited the sick, ministered to prisoners in Chester Castle, and catechized hundreds of children. 

Every morning at five o'clock, Henry rose to enter his study. He spent hours on his knees before the open Hebrew and Greek scriptures, not merely analyzing grammar, but communing with the Triune God. It was out of these early morning devotional hours that his immortal *Exposition of the Old and New Testaments* was born.

### 3. The Composition of the *Exposition* (1704–1714)
Beginning in 1704, Henry set out to publish a complete, verse-by-verse exposition of the entire Bible based on the Authorized King James text. He lived to complete the work from Genesis through the end of the Book of Acts before his sudden death from a stroke while on a preaching tour in 1714 at the age of fifty-one. Following his death, a circle of thirteen fellow Nonconformist ministers (including John Evans and William Tong) utilized Henry’s extensive sermon manuscripts and personal notes to complete the Epistles and Revelation.

## The Great Roster of Classic Biblical Commentators

While Matthew Henry holds pride of place for devotional and practical exposition, serious students of scripture should understand the unique contributions of the other classic commentators who form the heritage of the church:

### 1. John Gill (1697–1771): The Rabbinical Baptist Giant
John Gill, a legendary Strict Baptist pastor who preached in Southwark, London (preceding Charles Spurgeon’s pulpit), was one of the most astonishing linguists in church history. Gill was an expert in ancient Rabbinic Hebrew, the Targums, the Talmud, and the Midrash. His multi-volume *Exposition of the Old and New Testament* is unparalleled for uncovering Jewish historical context, ancient Second Temple traditions, and robust Reformed soteriology. Whenever you need to know how the ancient Jews understood a specific Old Testament idiom, John Gill is the definitive source.

### 2. Albert Barnes (1798–1870): The Pragmatic Exegete
Albert Barnes, an American Presbyterian minister who served in Philadelphia, authored *Notes on the Old and New Testaments*, which sold over one million copies in the nineteenth century. Barnes’s genius lay in his lucid, direct, verse-by-verse explanations. He avoids overly technical jargon, providing clear, common-sense grammatical explanations, geographical notes, and practical applications that make him exceptionally helpful for Sunday school teachers and lay leaders.

### 3. Jamieson-Fausset-Brown (JFB, 1871): The Doctrinal Benchmark
The *Commentary Critical and Explanatory on the Whole Bible* was a collaborative masterpiece by Robert Jamieson, A. R. Fausset, and David Brown. Published in Scotland, JFB is celebrated for its concise, punchy doctrinal clarity. It provides rigorous grammatical analysis of original Greek and Hebrew texts while summarizing profound theological conclusions in a fraction of the space required by larger commentaries.

### 4. Charles Ellicott (1819–1905): The Scholarly Bishop
Charles Ellicott, Bishop of Gloucester and Bristol and Chairman of the New Testament Revision Committee for the English Revised Version (1881), edited *An Old and New Testament Commentary for English Readers*. Ellicott’s work brings rigorous late-nineteenth-century British philological and historical scholarship, making it invaluable for resolving difficult textual and chronological questions.

### The Literary Genius of Matthew Henry: Aphorisms that Captured the English Soul

One of the reasons Matthew Henry remains so beloved is his unmatched capacity for **memorable, punchy aphorisms**—sentences so mathematically balanced and spiritually pungent that once heard, they can never be forgotten.

Perhaps the most famous aphorism in the entire history of biblical commentary is Henry’s exposition of Genesis 2:21–22 on the creation of woman from Adam’s rib:
> *"The woman was made of a rib out of the side of Adam; not made out of his head to rule over him, nor out of his feet to be trampled upon by him, but out of his side to be equal with him, under his arm to be protected, and near his heart to be beloved."*

Notice the extraordinary literary architecture: balanced clauses, contrasting parallel prepositions, and soaring pastoral beauty. For three centuries, pastors across the globe have quoted this single sentence during Christian wedding ceremonies.

Consider other immortal aphorisms scattered throughout Matthew Henry's exposition:
- **On Prayer:** *"You may as soon find a living man that does not breathe, as a living Christian that does not pray."*
- **On the Cross:** *"Christ took upon Him our sins, that we might put on His righteousness; He suffered death, that we might enjoy life; He descended into the grave, that we might ascend to glory."*
- **On Daily Devotion:** *"He that would be little in temptation must be much in prayer. The morning prayer is the golden key that opens the day, and the evening prayer is the iron bolt that shuts the night."*
- **On Trials:** *"Afflictions are God’s flails, to thresh off our husks; they are His pruning-knives, to cut away our withered branches; they are His furnace, to burn away our dross."*

## Four Golden Rules for Studying Commentaries

A biblical commentary is a servant, not a master. In the words of Martin Luther: *"Let the Christian reader not look to the commentaries, but look to the text itself."* 

To use Matthew Henry and classic commentaries with maximum spiritual profit on AduBible, always adhere to these four foundational rules:

### 1. Study the Biblical Text First Before Consulting Commentaries
Never open a commentary until you have spent at least ten minutes reading the scriptural passage directly in your parallel columns (e.g., NKJV + KJV + BSB). Observe the grammar, note repeated keywords, cross-reference synoptic passages, and pray for the illumination of the Holy Spirit. Form your own preliminary understanding. A commentary should be consulted to **test, refine, and enrich** your observations, not to replace your personal engagement with God's Word.

### 2. Distinguish Between Divine Scripture and Human Interpretation
The text of the Bible is the infallible, inerrant, breathed-out Word of the living God (2 Timothy 3:16). A commentary—no matter how saintly its author—is the fallible human reflection of an uninspired man. When Matthew Henry writes, he writes as a seventeenth-century English Nonconformist; when John Gill writes, he writes as an eighteenth-century Baptist. Always subject every human commentary to the supreme standard of scripture itself (*Sola Scriptura*).

### 3. Read for Spiritual Edification, Not Mere Intellectual Information
The unique glory of Matthew Henry is that he does not merely explain a verse; he **applies it to the heart**. When reading Henry, do not rush past his practical observations. Notice how he diagnoses the deceitfulness of human pride, exposes the subtlety of temptation, and magnifies the beauty of Jesus Christ. Read commentaries on your knees with an open heart, asking: *"Lord, how does this ancient truth confront my life today?"*

### 4. Cross-Examine Difficult Passages Across Multiple Voices
When wrestling with a notoriously difficult theological passage (such as Hebrews 6:4–6, Romans 9:18–24, or 1 Peter 3:19), do not rely upon a single commentator. Compare Matthew Henry alongside Jamieson-Fausset-Brown, John Gill, and modern lexical parsing on AduBible. Observing where great and godly scholars agree and where they differ prevents dogmatic sectarianism and cultivates theological humility.
